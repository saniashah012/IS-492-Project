Cossatin, A. G., Ferrero, F., Ardissono, L., & Mauro, N. (2026). The autonomy equation: How agentic AI reshapes trust and workload in routine productivity applications. Information Processing & Management, 63(5), 104681. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2026.104681


2.Structured Summary (4–6 sentences): Research Problem, Methodology, and Main Findings.

The central research question of this article is the problem of excessive email volume among workplace professionals and the resulting decline in work efficiency.  Traditional email systems cannot group similar tasks, and constantly switching between task types is extremely time-consuming. Manually labeling emails and creating to-do lists also requires extra effort. To address this issue, a team at Carnegie Mellon University developed an AI email assistant called RADAR. The system can help users shift from an email-centric workflow to a more efficient, task-centric one. The team conducted a controlled experiment with a large sample size, setting up a simulated meeting-planning test scenario. The final results showed that novices assisted by AI performed 37% better overall in email processing than those without assistance, made only one-quarter as many errors as the unassisted group, and were able to independently correct 89% of the AI's identification errors, demonstrating that the system effectively alleviates the burden of email overload.

3.Three Key Insights (3): Distinct principles or takeaways learned.

The study identified two design principles: First, to address the problem of email overload, we cannot simply keep patching old features. Adding folders and labels to emails is not enough; we must extract pending tasks hidden within scattered information, organize them by category, and display them separately, allowing users to batch-process similar items. Second, integrating AI learning technology and a task-centric interface into the email system can effectively alleviate the problem of email overload. However, this must be implemented gradually based on user needs: start by automating mechanical, repetitive tasks such as filling in fixed information, and only after users have adapted should the system provide priority recommendations and sorting suggestions. 

4.Two Limitations or Risks (2): Weaknesses, edge cases, or ethical/technical concerns identified.

In an effort to reduce the likelihood of misclassifying ordinary emails as tasks, the team tuned the AI to be overly cautious. As a result, in the final test, the classifier achieved an accuracy rate of 88.7%, but its recall rate was only 46.1%. This means that nearly half of the 102 actual to-do items in the test were missed, and the system was unable to recognize multiple similar tasks within the same email. In other words, if users rely entirely on the list generated by the AI, they are likely to miss important items.

5.One Concrete Inspiration (1): A specific design idea, system pattern, or feature this paper inspires for your project.
The specific design inspiration I gained from this study is that the project’s design software could include a feature that automatically scans class chat groups, provides links to the original messages, and automatically fills in standard information—such as names and student ID numbers—in forms, while sorting entries by deadline and priority. This would save everyone the trouble of scrolling through hundreds of chat messages to find notifications and ensure that no tasks are overlooked.
